Chapter 9: Muscles and Muscle Tissue

Slides 28 to 41: controlling the force of contraction, and how muscles respond to exercise.

What "contraction" means slide 29

Contraction = the force (tension) generating action of muscle fibers. The fiber does not have to shorten.

TypeWhat the fibers doExample
IsometricMake force, length stays the sameHolding a dumbbell still
ConcentricMake force and shortenCurling the dumbbell up
EccentricMake force and lengthenSlowly lowering it back down
Memory trickIso = same (length). Concentric = coming in (shorter). Eccentric = exiting out (longer).

The muscle twitch: 3 phases slide 30

PhaseWhat's happening
1. Latent periodE-C (excitation-contraction) coupling. No visible tension yet.
2. Period of contractionCross bridge cycling. Tension rises.
3. Period of relaxationCa2+ pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, cross bridges break.

What controls how much tension a muscle makes slides 31 to 33

Factor 1: how often action potentials (APs) arrive (these graphs are for single motor units)

  • A single stimulus does not reach maximum tension.
  • Stimuli that arrive before the muscle fully relaxes stack up and increase tension.
  • Very frequent stimuli reach maximum tension = tetanus. Tetanus can be unfused (still some wobble) or fused (smooth, steady max).

Factor 2: motor unit recruitment

  • Recruitment comes from a stronger signal from the brain.
  • Weakest signals turn on the motor units with the fewest fibers. Stronger signals add bigger units.
  • Henneman's size principle: small motor units are recruited first, then larger and larger ones.
  • Why it matters: fine control at low force (gently holding a baby's hand) and big jumps at high force (holding on for dear life), all with the same muscles.

Muscle tone slide 34

  • Tone = residual tension from a slight level of stimulus, even in resting muscle.
  • Tone is not definition. Good tone does not always mean visible definition.
  • Muscles used regularly develop more tone.

How muscles respond to exercise slides 35 to 37

The response depends on two things: 1) the muscle fiber type (how it makes ATP) and 2) the type of exercise.

ATP is made by phosphorylation of ADP (adding a phosphate to ADP).

Effects of training slides 40 and 41

Aerobic / endurance (cardio)Resistance (weights)
  • More capillaries, so better O2 and fuel delivery
  • More mitochondria, which use fuel and O2 to make ATP aerobically
  • More myoglobin, which stores O2
  • Hypertrophy (more muscle mass)
  • Fibers only rarely divide to make new fibers
  • Most growth = each fiber gets bigger: more fibrils are added, so the fiber's diameter increases

Chapter 10: The Muscular System

Concepts only. You don't need names of specific muscles (the muscle names below are just the slides' examples).

Muscles work in teams slides 4 and 5

  • Muscles can only pull. They cannot push.
  • So moving back and forth (like elbow flexion and extension) takes two muscles working against each other.
RoleWhat it does
Prime mover (= agonist)Provides most of the force
AntagonistOpposes the prime mover
SynergistSupporting role, helps the prime mover

Most motions use more than one muscle. Muscles rarely work alone.

Origin, insertion, and action slides 6 and 13

  • To move something, a muscle must span at least one joint.
  • Origin = the fixed attachment. Insertion = the attachment on the part that moves.
  • Action = the movement a muscle produces. Muscles pull in a straight line, so the action is what happens when the insertion is pulled toward the origin.
  • If you know the origin, insertion, and position, you can figure out the action.

Fascicle arrangements slides 7 to 10

Tension depends on the number of fibers, which depends on the muscle's size and its fascicle arrangement.

Chapter 11: The Nervous System

All slides: organization, glia, neurons, membrane and action potentials, synapses.

What the nervous system does slides 3 to 5

It monitors the inside and outside of the body and uses that information to control all the other systems, in 3 steps:

  1. Sensory input: sensors collect information
  2. Integration: the input is processed and a response is decided
  3. Motor output: the response is sent to an effector (muscle or gland)

The reflex arc is a simple example: sensation, integration, response.

How it's organized slides 6 and 7

CNS (central)PNS (peripheral)
Brain and spinal cord. In the dorsal cavity. Does integration.Cranial nerves and spinal nerves. The communication lines between the CNS and the body.
  • Sensory (afferent) division: carries signals toward the CNS.
  • Motor (efferent) division: carries signals away from the CNS to effectors. It splits into:
    • Somatic: voluntary, to skeletal muscle
    • Autonomic (ANS): involuntary, to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands. Splits into sympathetic (mobilizes the body during activity) and parasympathetic (conserves energy, housekeeping at rest).
Memory trickAfferent = Arrives at the CNS. Efferent = Exits the CNS.

Nervous tissue: neurons and glia slides 8 to 14

Neurons are excitable (send electrical signals) and do the "work." Neuroglia (glia) play supporting roles.

The neuron slides 15 to 17

Neurons have very long lifespans, are very long (axons can be over 1 m), and have very high metabolic rates.

PartJob
Cell bodyContains the nucleus
DendritesReceive signals
AxonConducts signals
Axon terminalsSend signals (secretory region)

Structural types (by number of processes): multipolar = many dendrites, one axon (most common). Bipolar = one dendrite, one axon. Unipolar = one process that acts as both.

Functional types: sensory neurons detect information and carry it to the CNS (afferent). Interneurons form the networks where integration happens. Motor neurons carry impulses to effectors (efferent).

Membrane potential and resting potential slides 19 to 23

  • Membrane potential = potential energy from a concentration gradient across a membrane. Because the gradient is made of ions, it's an electrochemical gradient.
  • It's built mostly by moving Na+ and K+ with ATP-powered Na+/K+ pumps.
  • The pump moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, leaving the outside positive and the inside negative.
  • K+ leakage channels are far more common than Na+ leakage channels, which adds to that charge difference.
  • Resting potential averages -70 mV.
Channel typeNormallyOpens when
LeakageAlways openAlways
Voltage gatedClosedA certain voltage is reached
Ligand gated (chemically gated)ClosedA specific chemical (like a neurotransmitter) attaches
Memory trick3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in: "3 for the street, 2 for home."

Changing the membrane potential slides 25 to 29

  • More Na+ permeability (Na+ rushes in) = depolarization (less negative).
  • More K+ permeability (K+ leaves) = hyperpolarization (more negative).
  • Graded potentials are local and short lived. A stronger stimulus makes a stronger one. They fade with distance.
  • Spatial summation = several different inputs at once add up. Temporal summation = repeated stimuli close together in time add up.
ReceptorDetects
MechanoreceptorsPressure (Pacinian and Meissner's corpuscles in skin)
ProprioceptorsMotion, position, stretch
ThermoreceptorsHot and cold
ChemoreceptorsChemicals (smell, taste, body chemistry)
PhotoreceptorsLight
NociceptorsTrauma, damage

The action potential (AP) slides 30 to 36

If a graded potential reaches threshold (-50 mV), an AP fires. An AP is all or nothing and travels the whole length of the axon.

StepWhat happens
1. Resting-70 mV
2. DepolarizationThreshold reached. Voltage gated Na+ channels open, Na+ rushes in
3. RepolarizationNa+ channels shut off. Voltage gated K+ channels open, K+ rushes out
4. HyperpolarizationK+ channels stay open a bit too long, dipping below -70
5. ResetNa+/K+ pumps restore resting potential
  • Once an AP starts, it cannot be stopped. It moves all the way to the axon terminal.
  • Every AP is the same size and length. A strong stimulus (a shout vs a whisper) makes more frequent APs, not bigger ones.
  • An AP lasts about 3 to 4 ms (depolarization plus repolarization is about 1.5 ms).
  • Absolute refractory period: no new AP is possible. This keeps the AP from moving backward.
  • Relative refractory period (hyperpolarization to the end): a new AP is possible but needs a stronger stimulus.
Memory trickNa+ in first, K+ out second. "Salt in, Potassium out."

Synapses slides 41 to 47

  • A synapse is where an axon terminal meets another cell. The gap is the synaptic cleft (between the presynaptic and postsynaptic cells).
  • Axons can synapse with dendrites (axodendritic), cell bodies (axosomatic), or rarely other axons (axoaxonal).
  • Transmission is usually chemical (neurotransmitter). Electrical is rare.
Chemical synapse in order
  1. AP reaches the axon terminal and opens voltage gated Ca2+ channels. Ca2+ enters.
  2. Ca2+ acts as a second messenger: vesicles of neurotransmitter (NT) fuse and dump NT into the cleft.
  3. NT binds ligand gated channels on the postsynaptic cell, causing a graded potential.
  4. NT is degraded or reabsorbed, so the postsynaptic cell can reset.
EPSP (excitatory)IPSP (inhibitory)
Opens Na+ channelsOpens K+ channels
Depolarizes, closer to thresholdHyperpolarizes, farther from threshold
Says "Fire!"Says "Don't fire!"

A neuron can get input from thousands of others. It responds to the sum of all the PSPs: opposite ones cancel out, and whichever type there's more of wins.
